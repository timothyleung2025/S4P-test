Real Boy/Real Girl

by Max Binder and Mo Christ

The puppeteers string me up. My elbows jump. My wrists hang limp in the air. Everything they want me to be: a girl, a boy.

Lovely to look at in my black dress, so handsome in my button-up. Tights, khaki pants, purple church shoes with a tiny kitten heel, sneakers caked in dirt after sliding at home base. I’m so good at playing the part.

To be girl is to obey, to be soft, ceramic tea kettle heavy over wispy blue flames. To be girl is to boil over and evaporate in the same breath, to love and to be loved, love silently.

To be boy is to get my way, to be a train boring through mountains, tracks paving my Manifest Destiny, to stand up straight with my hands up first.

To be gendered is to answer every question before it is asked, to ask no questions. I follow my cues. I say all the right lines. My body is a hollow instrument carved from broken branches, strings wrapped around my lungs, breathing in spite of me.

The puppeteer speaks, and my mouth jerks open. Speak only when I’m spoken for. Words coerced but called a gift. Control. Mimic. Shadow of a gender I wish I didn’t have to be. It’s just so easy to let someone else do the talking. Obedience is a safer body than mine.

His movements are my movements: run fast, walk with back straight, legs spread while seated, as if I were just the image in the mirror. And I let it happen. I learned by watching, another thread fastening me to womanhood, mimicking that which I must perform. I let my body be sculpted into caricature, each tug of the strings bringing a twinge of pain.

I can be a real girl and do what I’m told. I can be a real boy if it means survival. But when I look in the mirror, I cannot tell if it is my reflection or someone else’s gender. Gender is a puppet show. It’s ventriloquism, whatever it takes to fool you.

And there is no easy escape. Each time you call me he, boy, girl, I feel you tug the strings. Costumes letting my body escape me.

But I am learning how to become, how to fight through dysphoria and disobey, to untangle this body from the girl I was woven into, to unbow my joints from whittled blocks. I snapped the strings that have held me safe but sorry. They snap back and leave tally-mark bruises, one for every time I let femininity be replaced by whatever it is I’ve escaped to.

Change. Change isn’t easy when instruction has become reflex, performance a nervous habit, the kind that makes my bones stiff. Not a body, but a doll on a string.

I swallowed the graceful hand of God so that I might breathe this body whole. This is my resurrection. This is magic. This is truth. This is the only way I know how to be.

I wish my body didn’t need a gender. I wish I didn’t have to be a real boy or a real girl. I wish I didn’t have to be real to be allowed to exist. My body is still far from any kind of home. It still hurts to breathe too deeply, to be whole, but not yet holy.

Home is a body where you get to be you, no strings attached. Have you ever felt that? Have you?

Gender is a stage, and we are all fastened to it. Don’t be surprised when we break free.
